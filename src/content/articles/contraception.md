# Contraception

Contraception simply means ways people can prevent pregnancy when they are not ready to have children. Some methods also help protect people from sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV.

In many African communities, talking openly about contraception can feel uncomfortable because of culture, religion, or fear of judgment. But learning about family planning is important for protecting health, education, relationships, and future goals.

Contraception is not only for married people. Young people also have the right to accurate information and safe healthcare.

## Why Contraception Matters

Contraception can help:

- Prevent unplanned pregnancies
- Reduce unsafe abortions
- Protect young people from health risks
- Help girls stay in school
- Support healthy spacing between children
- Give couples and individuals more control over their future

For many young women in rural areas, access to contraception can mean the difference between continuing education and early pregnancy.

### Common Types of Contraception
- 1. Condoms

Condoms are thin covers worn during sex.

## Male Condoms

Worn on the penis.

## Female Condoms

Placed inside the vagina before sex.

- Benefits
- Help prevent pregnancy
- Protect against HIV and STIs
- Easy to get from clinics, pharmacies, and some youth centers
- Important
- Use a new condom every time
- Do not reuse condoms
- Store them in a cool, dry place
- 2. Birth Control Pills

These are pills taken daily by women to prevent pregnancy.

- Benefits
- Effective when taken correctly
- Can help regulate periods
- Challenges
- Must be taken every day
- Missing pills lowers protection
- 3. Injectable Contraceptives

An injection given at a clinic every 2–3 months.

- Benefits
- Private and long-lasting
- Does not require daily attention
- Possible Side Effects
- Changes in menstruation
- Weight changes
- Delayed return to fertility after stopping
- 4. Implants

Small flexible rods placed under the skin of the arm by a health worker.

- Benefits
- Works for 3–5 years
- Very effective
- Safe for many women
- Important
- Must be inserted and removed by trained providers
- 5. Intrauterine Device (IUD)

A small device placed inside the womb by a nurse or doctor.

- Benefits
- Long-term protection
- Can work for several years
- Important
- Requires clinic visit
- Does not protect against HIV/STIs
- 6. Emergency Contraception

Used after unprotected sex to reduce the chance of pregnancy.

- Important
- Works best within 72 hours
- Not meant for regular use
- Available at many clinics and pharmacies
- Myths vs Facts
- Myth:

- “Contraception makes women infertile forever.”

Fact:

Most contraceptives do NOT cause permanent infertility.

Myth:

- “Only married women should use family planning.”

Fact:

Anyone who is sexually active has the right to information and protection.

Myth:

- “Condoms are unsafe.”

Fact:

Condoms are safe and are one of the best ways to prevent HIV and STIs.

Myth:

- “Young people should not ask about contraception.”

Fact:

Young people deserve accurate health information without shame or fear.

## Where to Get Contraception

In many African countries, contraception can be found at:

- Government clinics
- Hospitals
- Pharmacies
- Youth-friendly health centers
- Community health workers
- Some NGOs and outreach programs

Some services may be free, especially at public health facilities.

## Your Rights

You have the right to:

- Ask questions privately
- Receive respectful treatment
- Access accurate SRHR information
- Make informed decisions about your body
- Seek healthcare without discrimination

No one should shame or abuse you for seeking health services.

## When to Seek Help

Talk to a health worker if:

- You experience unusual pain or bleeding
- You think your method is causing problems
- A condom breaks during sex
- You need advice on choosing the right method

### Common Side Effects of Contraceptives
- 1. Prolonged or Irregular Menstruation

This is very common with:

- Injectables
- Implants
- Some pills

A woman may experience:

- Bleeding for many days or weeks
- Spotting between periods
- Missed periods
- Heavy periods
- Why it happens

Hormonal contraceptives change the body’s natural hormone balance. The womb lining reacts differently, especially during the first few months.

- What can help
- Give the body time to adjust (often 3–6 months)
- Drink enough water and eat iron-rich foods
- Visit a clinic if bleeding becomes very heavy or causes weakness
- Seek medical help urgently if:
- Bleeding is extremely heavy
- You feel dizzy or faint
- You soak many pads in a short time
- Severe abdominal pain develops
- 2. Weight Gain

Some women notice:

- Increased appetite
- Water retention
- Slight body changes

This is more commonly reported with injectables like Depo-Provera, though not every woman experiences it.

- What can help
- Regular movement or exercise
- Balanced meals
- Monitoring sudden or extreme changes

Sometimes the weight gain is temporary. Sometimes the method simply doesn’t suit that person’s body.

## 3. Headaches

Mild headaches can happen when starting:

- Pills
- Implants
- Injectables
- Why

Hormones can affect blood vessels and body chemistry.

- What helps
- Rest
- Hydration
- Eating regularly
- Avoiding stress when possible
- Important

If headaches become:

- Severe
- Frequent
- Associated with blurred vision
- Associated with chest pain or weakness

…go to a health facility immediately. Those are not side effects to ignore.

## 4. Body Pains or Breast Tenderness

Some women report:

- Back pain
- Breast tenderness
- Muscle aches
- Lower abdominal discomfort

These are usually temporary while the body adjusts.

- What helps
- Rest
- Light exercise
- Warm compress
- Pain relief advised by a health worker
- 5. Mood Changes

Hormonal contraception can sometimes affect emotions.

Some women experience:

- Mood swings
- Irritability
- Anxiety
- Low mood

This is real and should not be dismissed.

If someone feels emotionally overwhelmed after starting a method, they should speak to a nurse or doctor about alternatives.

## One Important Thing People Don’t Talk About Enough

A lot of women are given contraception without proper counseling.

Sometimes a woman gets an injection at a clinic and nobody explains:

- Possible side effects
- Warning signs
- Alternative methods
- What to do if problems start

That creates fear and mistrust.

Good family planning should always involve:

- Clear explanation
- Choice
- Consent
- Follow-up support
- What Needs To Be Done
- 1. Don’t Suffer Quietly

Many women stay silent because they fear judgment or think side effects are “normal.”

If symptoms are affecting daily life, go back to the clinic.

## 2. Change the Method if Necessary

Not every contraceptive suits every woman.

A nurse may suggest:

- Switching from injectable to implant
- Trying a lower-dose pill
- Using non-hormonal methods like condoms or copper IUD
- 3. Attend Follow-Up Visits

Especially during the first months after starting a method.

## 4. Get Proper Medical Advice

Avoid relying only on community rumors or social media stories.

Some myths exaggerate side effects, while some real complications are ignored.

## Dangerous Signs That Need Immediate Medical Attention

Go to a clinic or hospital urgently if there is:

- Severe chest pain
- Difficulty breathing
- Very severe headaches
- Loss of vision
- Swelling in one leg
- Extremely heavy bleeding
- Fainting
- Severe abdominal pain

These are rare, but important.

## The Bigger Picture

Contraception should improve a woman’s life, not make it harder.

The best approach is:

- informed choice,
- regular support,
- honest conversations,
and access to different options.

Women deserve full information before choosing any method.
